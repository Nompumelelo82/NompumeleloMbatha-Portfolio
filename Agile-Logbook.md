

# Agile Logbook

## What an Agile Logbook Is
An Agile logbook is a record of what the team planned, what it completed and what is coming next. For SafeGuard Soshanguve, this logbook combines our daily stand-up entries, written in our logbook template, with the sprint record on our Trello board. The board covers all 16 sprints, from 20 July to 30 October 2026. On Trello every card has an owner and a due date, and each finished card sits in our "Sprint | Completed" column. Owners are shown by their Trello initials, and the dates are the due dates on the cards.

## How We Logged Our Work
At the start of each sprint we selected about five cards from the Product Backlog and gave each one an owner and a due date. At our stand-ups we answered the same three questions: what did I complete since the last stand-up, what am I planning to complete between now and the next stand-up, and what are my impediments, risks or problems. We recorded the answers in our logbook template, which also has a taskboard (Product Backlog, In Progress, Completed) and a walkthrough of four team questions. The sections below show our entries for 4 to 7 August and for the final week, followed by the log of all 16 sprints.

## Reflection

**What I did.** For SafeGuard Soshanguve, our team kept a record of our work in two ways. We wrote daily stand-up entries in our Agile logbook template, answering the same three questions each time, and we tracked the sprints on Trello, with about five cards per sprint, each with an owner and a due date. Together they cover the project from 20 July to 30 October, over 16 sprints. This page shows our entries for 4 to 7 August, our entries for the final week, and a log of every sprint in between.

**What I learned about keeping a log.** A logbook makes progress visible. Our 4 to 7 August entries show how a week moves from one day to the next. On Tuesday we went through the product backlog and started updating outdated cards, by Wednesday we had finished the backlog and started drafting the brand guide, on Thursday we presented it to our lecturer and received feedback, and on Friday we finalised it. Each "planned next" answer became the next day's "completed" answer, so the log proves we did what we said we would. It also made me more accountable, because I had to say what I would do before the next stand-up and then report on it.

**What I learned about the walkthrough and taskboard.** The template does more than the daily questions. The taskboard (Product Backlog, In Progress, Completed) shows where each piece of work stands, and the walkthrough asks four questions about how to advance the work, whether anyone is working outside the board, what the team still needs to finish, and whether anything is blocking the flow. Asking "is anyone working on anything that is not on the board?" every sprint reinforced our agreement to keep one source of truth, and it stopped hidden work from building up.

**What I learned about planning in small steps.** Because each sprint was about five cards, the work always felt manageable. A product with more than 60 features could have felt overwhelming, but a week at a time it was clear what mattered now. The sprint log shows the product grow in a clear order. We began with the technical foundation in Sprint 1, then let residents register and set up emergency contacts in Sprint 2. Guards joined in Sprint 3, and in Sprint 4 the SOS button brought everything together. After that, each sprint added a layer: updates during an alert, history and ratings, community safety features, guard tools, admin tools, payments, security and testing. The order matters, because the SOS button needed residents and guards first, and the admin tools for approving guards needed guards to upload their certificates first.

**How feedback showed up in the log.** The week of 4 to 7 August shows feedback working as it should. We presented the brand guide to our lecturer on Thursday, wrote down the feedback, and applied it the same day, and by Friday the guide was finalised. That taught me that a sprint is not only about finishing work, it is about showing it early enough to improve it.

**How the log shows teamwork.** The initials next to every card show that the work was shared across the whole team. Some cards had more than one owner, such as the onboarding walkthrough in Sprint 15, which was shared between three people. A logbook is not only about tracking tasks, it is also about showing who is responsible for what, so no one is unsure and no one carries the whole project.

**Quality, security and launch.** The later entries show a shift from building features to making the product safe and ready. Sprint 13 added rate limiting and error logging, Sprint 14 added unit tests, a POPIA compliance review and penetration testing, and Sprint 16 added load testing, crash reporting and the app store deployment pipeline. I learned that finishing a product means testing it, protecting people's data and preparing it for release, and that these tasks need their own place in the plan.

**What was difficult.** Our sprints were not all the same size. Sprint 1 had six cards and Sprint 15 only two, and some cards were much bigger than others. Keeping the log honest was also a challenge, because it only helps if the record matches what really happened and is updated as we go, not written afterwards. My entries also say "None" under impediments for the whole week of 4 to 7 August. That is true, but it means the log does not show how we solve problems, and I would like future entries to record not only whether something blocked us but what we did about it.

**How it connects to the rest of my portfolio.** The logbook records the work that comes out of the other four pieces. Our Agile Team Charter set the rules for how we worked, including the stand-up questions and that every task has an owner. The user stories and Product Backlog describe what we built, and the Sprint Backlog shows the cards we chose for each sprint. The logbook is where those pieces come together as a timeline of what we actually finished.

**What I would do differently.** Next time I would add a short retrospective at the end of every sprint to record what went well, what did not, and what to change. I would write a clear sprint goal at the start of each sprint and check it at the end, and I would note decisions and how we solved problems in the log as they happen. This work improved my organisation, my communication with the team, and my ability to track a large project from the first day to the last.

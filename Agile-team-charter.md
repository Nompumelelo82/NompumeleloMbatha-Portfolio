## What This Charter Is
This charter is our team's social contract. We created it together to reduce friction, so that everyone knows how we work, and to align our daily execution, so that each person knows what to do next. It is a living document, and we review it as we learn what works for our team.

## Vision Statement
To make Soshanguve a safer community by giving residents an affordable, locally run security service backed by a mobile alert app for fast emergency response.

## Shared Purpose
Crime is a serious problem in Soshanguve, and many residents cannot afford big private security companies. Our team set out to build SafeGuard, a community-based security solution that uses local guards and a mobile alert system, priced at R100 to R200 a month. We committed to testing our idea with real community members before building the full product. Everything we build is meant to give residents a faster response in an emergency and to give local guards the tools to respond.

## Core Values
- **Respect:** every team member's ideas are heard, even when we disagree.
- **Honesty:** we say early when we are stuck or behind.
- **Accountability:** if we commit to a card, we finish it or tell the team in time.
- **Collaboration:** we share the work and help each other instead of working alone.
- **Community focus:** decisions are made with the people we are building for in mind, the residents and guards of Soshanguve.
- **Safety and trust:** because SafeGuard handles people's names and locations in emergencies, we protect their data and test our work before we call it done.

## Working Agreements
- **One board, one source of truth:** all work lives on our Trello board. Each sprint is a column with about five cards, and finished cards move to our Sprint | Completed column.
- **Every feature is a user story:** we write each card in the format "As a [user], I want [feature] so that [benefit]" in its description.
- **Every story has tasks:** each story has a checklist of smaller tasks, such as designing the screen, building the feature and testing it.
- **Every card has an owner and a due date:** the owner is shown by their initials, and the due date is set when the card is planned into a sprint. Some cards are shared by more than one person.
- **Stand-ups:** during each sprint, every member answers three questions: what did I complete since the last stand-up, what am I planning to complete next, and what is blocking me. We record this in our Agile Logbook.
- **Communication:** WhatsApp for quick updates and reminders, Microsoft Teams for meetings and sharing files, and Trello for tracking work.
- **Decisions:** we discuss as a team and agree together before moving on.
- **Documentation:** our product backlog, sprint backlog and logbook are kept up to date so anyone can see what has been done and what comes next.

## Operational Boundaries
- **Sprint size:** we take on about five cards per sprint, so that the workload stays realistic and finishable.
- **Definition of done:** a card is done only when every task on its checklist is ticked off, including testing, and then it moves to Completed.
- **Scope:** we build for the SafeGuard Soshanguve community, and new ideas go into the Product Backlog and wait for a sprint instead of being added in the middle of one.
- **Legal and safety requirements:** guards must be PSIRA-registered, and we review our work against POPIA before launch.
- **Missing a meeting or deadline:** the team member tells the team beforehand, catches up using the logbook and Trello, and the team offers help or reassigns the card if needed.
- **Conflict:** we talk about the issue, not the person. If the team cannot solve it, we ask our lecturer to help.
- **Fairness:** everyone contributes, and no one carries the whole team.

## A Living Document
This charter is not written once and forgotten. When something causes friction, such as unclear cards, uneven sprint sizes or late tasks, we change our agreements and update this page. Our lecturer's feedback on validating our idea with the community is one example. It changed our plan, and we then tested our assumptions with surveys and our Instagram page @safeguard.soshanguve.

## Reflection

**What I did.** At the start of SafeGuard Soshanguve, our team agreed how we would work together and wrote it into this charter. It covers why we are building the product, the values we share, the rules we follow day to day, and the limits we work within. The definition of a charter calls it a "social contract", and that is what it became for us: a single page we could point to when anyone was unsure what was expected.

**What I learned.** A team is not only about the product. It is also about agreeing on how we work together. Our working agreements are simple, but they shaped the whole project. Putting everything on one Trello board, writing every card as a user story with a checklist, and giving every card an owner and a due date meant that nobody had to ask who was doing what. When I look at our board now, with 16 sprints and an owner on every card, I can see those agreements working in practice. The charter is what made the board consistent.

**Why the values mattered.** The value I found most important was community focus. We are a team of students building for people in Soshanguve, and it is easy to build what is interesting to us instead of what residents need. After our lecturer's feedback, we stopped assuming that residents would pay R100 to R200 a month and tested it with surveys and an Instagram page. That is community focus turned into action. I also saw why we added safety and trust as a value. SafeGuard handles names and locations during emergencies, and that is why cards like the POPIA compliance review, penetration testing and rate limiting on the SOS endpoint are on our board.

**Why the boundaries mattered.** The operational boundaries reduced friction more than I expected. Limiting each sprint to about five cards kept our workload realistic. Defining "done" as every checklist task ticked off, including testing, gave us a shared standard. Keeping new ideas in the backlog instead of adding them mid-sprint protected our sprints from constant change. Without these limits, we would have argued about the same things again and again.

**What was difficult.** The hardest part was that a charter only works if the team actually follows it. It is easy to agree on rules at the start and then forget them under deadline pressure. Our board shows that sprints were not always the same size, some cards were much bigger than others, and a few cards had to be shared between people. I realised that our agreements about workload and testing were good, but we did not always check that every checklist included a test task. I also found it difficult to be specific. Agreements like "tell the team early" are hard to measure, so I would write them with clearer numbers next time.

**How it connects to the rest of my portfolio.** The charter is the base of the other four pieces. Our user stories and tasks follow the story format we agreed on. The Product Backlog and Sprint Backlog follow our agreement of one board, about five cards per sprint, and an owner and due date on every card. The Agile Logbook records the stand-ups where we reported on our work using the three agreed questions. The charter explains why those other pieces look the way they do.

**What I would do differently.** Next time I would review the charter at the end of every sprint, not only when a problem appears, and I would record each change so there is evidence that it is a living document. I would set exact response times in our group chat, agree on a clear step to follow when someone falls behind, and add a testing task to every card as a rule. I would also invite community members to comment on our shared purpose and values, since we are building for them. This process improved my teamwork, my communication and my understanding of how an Agile team stays organised.

# User Stories and Tasks

## Project
SafeGuard Soshanguve: a community security service with local guards and a mobile alert app for residents of Soshanguve.

## Who We Wrote the Stories For
Our main user is based on our customer persona, Andile Gomo. He is 35, works hard on a limited income, relies on public transport, and worries about crime at home and in the community. He wants affordable security (R100 to R200 a month) and a fast response in an emergency.

## How We Wrote Them
Each user story follows the format: "As a [type of user], I want [a feature] so that [the benefit]." We then broke every story into smaller tasks and tracked them on our Trello board, so each task could be assigned, moved across the board and finished within a sprint.

## User Stories

### Story 1: Emergency alert
As a resident, I want to send an emergency alert with one tap so that help can reach me quickly.

Tasks:
- Design the panic button screen
- Build the alert button and confirmation message
- Send the alert to the guards and community group
- Test the alert flow

### Story 2: Crime alerts nearby
As a resident, I want to receive alerts when crime happens near me so that I can stay safe and informed.

Tasks:
- Design the alerts feed screen
- Set up community alert notifications
- Test that alerts arrive on time

### Story 3: Affordable subscription
As a household head, I want a clear and affordable monthly fee so that I know what I am paying for and can budget for it.

Tasks:
- Define the R100 to R200 pricing options
- Design the subscription and payment screen
- Validate the price with the community through our survey and Instagram page

### Story 4: Local guard response
As a security guard, I want to receive alerts with the resident's location so that I can respond fast.

Tasks:
- Design the guard alert screen
- Show the alert location to the guard
- Let the guard mark an alert as responded to

### Story 5: Community trust
As a community member, I want to see what SafeGuard offers before I sign up so that I can decide whether to trust it.

Tasks:
- Create the Instagram page @safeguard.soshanguve
- Post the app screens with a call to action (comment YES)
- Track likes, comments, shares and followers (target: 50 followers)

### Story 6: Youth employment
As a young person in Soshanguve, I want to know about guard job opportunities so that I can apply and earn an income.

Tasks:
- Add a short question about guard interest to our survey
- Share the opportunity with youth on the Instagram page

## Reflection

**What I did.** For SafeGuard Soshanguve, our team wrote user stories from the point of view of the people who would use the service. We based them on our customer persona, Andile Gomo, a hardworking 35-year-old who is worried about crime and cannot afford expensive private security. We then broke each story into smaller tasks and put them on our Trello board so that every task had an owner and could move from To Do, to In Progress, to Done.

**What I learned.** Before this, we described SafeGuard by listing features, such as an alert button or a subscription fee. Writing the stories in the format "As a [user], I want [feature] so that [benefit]" forced me to ask why each feature matters. The "so that" part was the most useful, because it connects every feature to a real need. For example, the emergency alert is not just a button. It exists because residents fear slow police response. Using Andile as a reference made our decisions clearer, and it helped us see which stories were most important to build first.

**Why breaking stories into tasks mattered.** A story like "send an emergency alert" is too big to start on. Once we split it into tasks like designing the panic button screen, building the alert, and testing the flow, it became something one person could finish within a sprint. Trello made progress visible to everyone, so nobody had to ask who was doing what. This also supported our team charter, where we agreed that every task must have an owner.

**What was difficult.** The hardest part was knowing how detailed to make each story. If a story was too big, it was unclear when it was finished. If it was too small, we ended up with a long list that was hard to manage. I also found it easy to write stories that were really just our own assumptions about what the community wants.

**How feedback changed my thinking.** Our lecturer's feedback pointed out that we had assumed residents would pay R100 to R200 a month without checking. That taught me that some stories are assumptions that must be tested. We turned the pricing story into tasks to validate it through our survey and our Instagram page @safeguard.soshanguve, where we set a target of 50 followers to measure real interest. I now understand that a good user story is not only a feature idea, it is something you can test with real users.

**How it connects to the rest of my portfolio.** The user stories are the starting point for the rest of our Agile work. They feed into our Product Backlog, where we prioritise them, and into our Sprint Backlog, where we choose which ones to build in each sprint. Our Agile Logbook then records our progress on them during stand-ups, and our team charter explains the rules we followed while working on them.

**What I would do differently.** Next time I would add acceptance criteria to every story, so the whole team agrees on what "done" means before we start. I would also involve real users earlier, when writing the stories, instead of relying only on our persona. This experience has improved my planning, my ability to think from the user's point of view, and my confidence working in an Agile team.
